# Sharing the productivity gains: an AI workforce transition package

> **Policy client and context:** Deputy Secretary, Skills and Training Group, Australian Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR), advising the 2026 workforce implementation of the *National AI Plan*, especially Action 5, “Support and train Australians”.

## Executive summary

Generative AI (GenAI) is more likely to reorganise Australian jobs than eliminate them, but gains will not be automatic or evenly shared. Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) finds greater potential for task augmentation than automation, while routine clerical work, entry-level pathways and already disadvantaged cohorts face concentrated risks. DEWR should implement a three-year **AI Workforce Transition Package**: targeted AI Skills Accounts; a Transition Guarantee for workers affected by AI-related restructuring; and matched grants for worker-centred adoption in small and medium enterprises (SMEs). The package would cost **$420 million over three years**. It links training to real job redesign, protects mobility and wages during adjustment, and diffuses productivity beyond large firms. Funding should be staged, independently evaluated and conditional on worker consultation, privacy safeguards and measurable employment, wage and productivity outcomes.

## Problem definition and policy context

The *National AI Plan* directs DEWR and the Department of Education to explore AI-ready skills and credentials while keeping workplace rights fit for purpose (Department of Industry, Science and Resources (DISR) 2025). This proposal supplies the labour-market delivery mechanism.

The relevant unit is the **task**, not the job title. GenAI can automate or augment tasks and induce workflow redesign. JSA’s illustrative modelling assumes automation of 13% of tasks and augmentation of about 55%; this is a scenario, not a forecast (JSA 2025). Adoption and demand determine whether saved time produces more or better output, shorter hours or fewer workers. Policy should insure concentrated losses while enabling experimentation, not preserve every task.

Australia had 14.84 million people employed and 4.6% unemployment in August 2026 (Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) 2026). Waiting for aggregate job losses would be late because skills and firm capabilities take time to build.

## Labour-market impacts

**Employment and tasks.** JSA (2025) finds no evidence yet of widespread Australian displacement. High-skill knowledge jobs are mainly augmentable, while automation potential is concentrated in routine clerical and administrative tasks. Entry-level roles may survive but lose the routine work through which novices learn, making supervised practice and judgment more important. Over time, employment is likely to shift from some clerical and professional roles towards trades, care and community-service work, while new AI-complementary roles emerge.

**Wages and job quality.** If augmentation raises output that customers value, labour demand and wages can rise. If demand is fixed, the same output may require fewer hours, putting downward pressure on employment or wages. Bargaining power therefore matters. Algorithmic hiring, rostering and performance management can also increase surveillance, bias and work intensity. Consultation, human review and privacy safeguards are productivity institutions, not merely compliance costs: workers hold the tacit knowledge required to redesign tasks safely.

**Distribution.** JSA finds women slightly more exposed in Australia because they are concentrated in clerical roles; the ILO’s broader 2026 evidence similarly finds 29% of workers in female-dominated occupations exposed, versus 16% in male-dominated occupations (International Labour Organization (ILO) 2026). Mature-age workers, people with disability, First Nations people and remote communities may also face digital-access or training barriers. Untargeted subsidies would therefore risk financing already advantaged professional users.

**Industrial structure.** Adoption can shift market share towards firms with data, capital and digital management capability. Only 29% of regional organisations were adopting AI compared with 40% in metropolitan areas (DISR 2025). The Productivity Commission (2024) argues Australia’s near-term advantage lies mainly in adapting general models to local uses, which makes diffusion, interoperable data and complementary skills more important than subsidising frontier-model development. Without SME support, AI could widen both productivity and regional gaps.

## Recommended package and implementation

DEWR should fund all three options as a **$420 million, three-year package**, with continuation conditional on demonstrated results.

| Timing | Delivery and responsibility | Decision points and KPIs |
|---|---|---|
| **Months 0–6** | DEWR leads design with DISR/NAIC, JSA, Jobs and Skills Councils, states, unions and employer groups. JSA identifies priority occupations and establishes employment, wage, task and firm-size baselines. | Publish eligibility, approved-course and consultation rules; establish privacy-preserving data linkage and an independent evaluator. |
| **Months 7–18** | Pilot 20,000 Skills Accounts, 5,000 Guarantees and 500 SME grants across financial/professional services, health administration and regional businesses. | Report quarterly on participation, completions, job quality, displacement, earnings and productivity; compare outcomes with matched non-participants. |
| **Months 19–36** | Scale to the stated ceilings only where interim evaluation shows additional benefits; review at month 30 and sunset ineffective components. | 80,000 accounts with 75% completion and 70% going to priority cohorts; 70% of Guarantee users re-employed within six months and median earnings at least 90% of previous earnings within 12 months; 2,000 SME projects, 40% regional, with 75% reporting independently verifiable time, quality or service gains without worse worker outcomes. |

## Risks and mitigations

Training may subsidise activity that employers would have funded or reward weak providers. Targeting, employer-paid release time, approved credentials and holding back 20% of provider payment until completion limit this risk. Firms may label ordinary restructuring “AI-related”; access to the Guarantee should instead be triggered by a documented material technology restructure, not disputed causation. Grants could enable surveillance, bias or vendor lock-in. Mandatory worker consultation, privacy and work-health-and-safety checks, human appeal channels, data portability and grant clawbacks address these harms. Finally, uncertain effects require staged funding, matched comparison groups and a public JSA dashboard rather than permanent programs based on exposure estimates alone.
